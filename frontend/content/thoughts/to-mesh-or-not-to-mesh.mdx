> **TL;DR:** A service mesh is a layer of small proxies that sits between your services and handles how they talk to each other: it shows you the traffic, encrypts it, and lets you steer it, all without changing your application code. It's great when you have many services and need those things everywhere. For a small setup it's often more cost and complexity than it's worth. I ran one on my cluster, learned a lot, and then turned it off.

![Title Image: To mesh or not to mesh](/images/thoughts/to-mesh-or-not-to-mesh/to-mesh-or-not-to-mesh.png)

A while ago I got curious about service meshes. A fair question is why someone running a small blog needs one. The short answer: **FAFO.**

The longer answer goes back to 2020. I was on call at a large e-commerce company and was told to "check the Istio logs". I nodded, had no idea what Istio was, and after a while it felt too late to ask. Years later I finally had enough of the fundamentals to dig in properly. This post is the explanation I wish I'd had back then.

## What is a service mesh?

Picture a big office building where every team keeps passing notes to other teams. A service mesh is like putting a small mail room next to every team. Teams still write the notes, but the mail rooms handle delivery: they log every note that goes in and out, seal each one so nobody can read it on the way, retry if the other team didn't pick up, and stop sending to a team that's clearly overwhelmed.

In Kubernetes terms, the "teams" are your services and the "mail rooms" are proxies. Each proxy sits next to a service and handles its network traffic. A central **control plane** tells all the proxies what to do.

The key idea is that your services don't change. You get these features by changing the network around them, not the code inside them.

## The problem it solves

![Service mesh architecture](/images/thoughts/to-mesh-or-not-to-mesh/service-mesh.png)

Most of the traffic in a microservices app never leaves the cluster. Take an e-commerce product page: to render it, the page service calls a product service, a reviews service, a ratings service, a coupons service, and so on. This internal service-to-service traffic is called **east-west traffic** (as opposed to north-south, which comes in from users).

Once you have dozens of services calling each other, some questions get hard to answer:

- **Who is calling whom**, how often, and how many of those calls fail?
- **Is that traffic encrypted**, and can a service prove it's really who it says it is?
- **Can I control it?** Retry failed calls, send 10% of traffic to a new version, or stop calling a service that's falling over.

You can build all of this into every service yourself, in every language you use. A service mesh gives it to you once, for everything.

## What you get

### See the traffic (observability)

This is arguably the biggest win. Because every request passes through a proxy, the mesh can tell you, for every service: requests per second, success and error rates, and how long calls take. Many meshes also produce a live map of which services talk to which. Paired with a tracing tool like Jaeger, you can follow one slow request across five services and see exactly where the time went.

### Lock it down (mutual TLS)

Normal HTTPS proves the *server's* identity to the client. **Mutual TLS (mTLS)** goes further: both sides prove who they are, and everything between them is encrypted. A mesh sets this up for you, including issuing and rotating a certificate for every workload. In Linkerd, for example, a root certificate signs an intermediate one, which signs a short-lived certificate for each pod.

Worth knowing: if encryption is your *only* reason for a mesh, check what you already have. Your cloud provider or load balancer may already encrypt traffic where it matters, and you may not need pod-to-pod encryption at all.

### Steer it (traffic management)

Because the proxies understand HTTP, you can make decisions based on it:

- **Canary releases:** send 10% of traffic to a new version and 90% to the old one, then shift gradually.
- **Retries and timeouts:** retry a failed call automatically, and give up after a set time instead of hanging.
- **Circuit breakers:** if a service starts failing a lot, stop calling it for a while so it can recover, instead of piling on and causing a chain reaction.
- **Smarter load balancing:** spread calls across pods based on how they're actually responding.

Kubernetes' built-in network policies can already allow or block traffic by IP and port, but they can't look at HTTP paths or headers. That's where a mesh, or a smarter networking layer like Cilium, comes in.

## How meshes work: three approaches

All meshes do roughly the same job. The big difference is *where* the proxy lives.

| Approach | Where the proxy lives | Examples | Good at | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sidecar** | A small proxy container inside every pod | Linkerd, Istio (classic) | Mature, isolated per pod, full HTTP features | A proxy per pod adds up in memory and CPU |
| **Per-node** | One shared proxy per node, plus optional HTTP proxies | Istio Ambient | Much lower overhead, no pod restarts to adopt | An extra hop, and one proxy failing affects the whole node |
| **In the kernel** | eBPF programs inside the Linux kernel | Cilium | Fast, combines networking, policies and mesh | Harder to debug, needs a recent kernel |

A bit more on each:

- **Sidecars** are the classic design. Istio's sidecar is Envoy, which is powerful but relatively heavy. Linkerd uses its own small proxy written in Rust, which is why I picked it for a small cluster.
- **Istio Ambient mode** (generally available since 2024) drops the per-pod sidecar. A shared proxy on each node handles encryption and basic metrics for every pod. You add heavier HTTP-aware proxies only for the services that need them.
- **Cilium** does much of the work inside the Linux kernel using eBPF, so for many features there's no separate proxy at all. It's also your cluster's networking layer, which makes it one tool instead of two.

## The catch

Everything you add to your platform has a cost:

- **Resources.** A proxy next to every pod uses memory and CPU. On a big cluster that's real money; on a small one, it can be a big share of what you have. Ambient mode and Cilium shrink this a lot.
- **Complexity.** It's another system to install, upgrade, debug and understand. When a request fails, "is it my app or the mesh?" becomes a new question.
- **Expertise.** Someone on the team has to know it well, or it becomes the thing nobody wants to touch.

So the honest question is: *am I adding this because everyone does, or because my setup needs it?*

## So, do you need one?

**You probably don't if:**
- you have a handful of services that don't call each other much
- network policies plus your API gateway already cover what you need
- your cluster is small and every bit of memory counts
- nobody on the team wants to own another system

**You should consider one if:**
- you run lots of microservices that call each other constantly
- you need canaries, retries and circuit breakers across many services
- you need the same metrics and tracing everywhere, without asking every team to add them
- compliance requires encrypted, authenticated traffic between services

**And there's a middle ground:**
- **Istio Ambient** if you want mesh features with much lower overhead
- **Cilium** if you want networking, policies and many mesh features in one tool
- **Just the pieces you need,** such as network policies for access control and a library or gateway for retries

## What I ended up doing

I ran Linkerd on my homelab cluster for a while, and it taught me exactly what I wanted to learn: how proxies intercept traffic, how mTLS certificates are issued and rotated, and what the traffic between my services actually looks like.

Then, in October 2026, I removed it. My cluster is three small servers, and memory was tight. For a blog and a few side projects, the mesh was costing more than it gave back. That's the answer to this post's question, at least for my setup: a service mesh is a great tool when you have the scale and the problems it solves. When you don't, it's a fantastic thing to learn, and a fine thing to turn off.
